Guardian Pipeline

It writes a protocol's mathematical safety rules once, then enforces that exact same definition on both sides of a deployment — proven by a fuzzing campaign before the code goes live, and re-checked by a live monitor after it does.

That's the whole idea. Everything else in this page — the vault, the 12 invariants, the fuzzer, the monitor, the score — is detail in service of that one sentence. This explainer builds it up from zero, assuming you know how to think but not necessarily what a "collateral ratio" or a "fuzz campaign" is. Several diagrams below are interactive — poke them.

Chapter 01

The problem: an audit is a photograph

Smart contracts hold real money and, once deployed, are very hard to change. A bug isn't a crash — it's a theft. So how do people try to make them safe today?

The dominant answer is the audit: you pay experts to read your code and write a report. That's genuinely useful, but it has a structural weakness that this whole project is a response to.

Weakness 1 — it's a snapshot

An audit attests that the code looked sound on the day it was signed. It can't speak to the parameters, the market prices, or the state the contract drifts into months later. The photograph is accurate; the world keeps moving.

Weakness 2 — the two sides drift

Teams usually test safety before launch with one tool, and monitor after launch with a different tool — often checking differently-written rules. So "the thing we proved" and "the thing we watch" can quietly disagree. Nobody notices until it's too late.

Guardian Pipeline's bet

Write the safety properties once. Enforce that identical definition in two places: a fuzzing campaign that attacks the code before deploy, and a runtime monitor whose checks mirror that campaign function-for-function after deploy. If the two ever disagree, the project has failed its own thesis — so they're deliberately built to be the same maths, sharing the same names (INV-01 … INV-12).

The framing is informed by two empirical papers on the DeFi audit market (Bourveau et al. 2024; Landsman et al. 2025), which find that audits are widespread but their effectiveness is an open question. Continuous, two-sided verification is this project's proposed answer — not a claim from those papers.

Chapter 02

What is a lending vault, exactly?

To have something concrete to protect, the project ships a small lending vault. You don't need DeFi background — here's the entire mental model in five pieces.

Two assets
There are two different tokens. The debt asset is what lenders supply and borrowers borrow (think: a stablecoin). The collateral asset is a separate token borrowers lock up as security (think: ETH).
Lenders
Deposit the debt asset and receive shares. A share is a claim on the pool. As borrowers pay interest, the pool grows, so each share is redeemable for more than was put in. Lenders earn yield as a rising share price.
Borrowers
Lock collateral, then borrow the debt asset against it — but only up to 80% of the collateral's value (the collateral ratio). Borrowing more than your collateral backs is forbidden. This "over-collateralisation" is what protects lenders.
Interest
A borrower's debt grows every second via a borrow index — a multiplier that only ever ticks up. accrue() charges borrowers and credits the exact same amount to lenders. Both sides move together, by design.
Oracle
The vault needs to know the collateral's price. It reads an external price oracle. If a borrower's debt grows past 80% of their (re-priced) collateral, their position is under-water and anyone may liquidate it: repay the debt, seize the collateral, keep a small bonus.
The one number that must never lie

Solvency. The vault must always hold (or be owed) at least as much as it owes its lenders. In symbols:

cash + totalBorrowed ≥ totalSupplyAssets

Idle cash, plus money out on loan, covers every lender's claim. If this is ever false, lenders cannot all be paid back — the protocol is insolvent. Keep this inequality in mind; it's INV-01, the master rule.

What to notice

After Bob borrows and time passes, his debt grows (interest) and Alice's redeemable value grows by the exact same amount — that's accrue() moving both sides together. The solvency rule never goes red, because every operation is rounded in the protocol's favour. When ETH crashes, Bob goes under-water and liquidation becomes possible — yet even then the vault stays solvent. That property holding across every sequence is exactly what the next chapters prove.

Chapter 04

Invariants: the core idea

An invariant is a statement that must be true in every state the system can ever reach — not "true for this input", but "true always, no matter what anyone does in what order".

A normal test says "if I deposit 100, I get 100 shares." That checks one path. An invariant says "no matter what sequence of deposits, borrows, repays, liquidations, price moves and time jumps happen — the vault is still solvent." That's a vastly stronger claim, and it's the right shape for safety, because attackers don't follow the happy path.

Why this framing is powerful

  • It's machine-checkable. An invariant is just a boolean over the contract's public state. A computer can evaluate it after any action.
  • It's the same before and after deploy. The same boolean can be fuzzed in CI and evaluated against a live chain. That's the whole bridge.
  • Violations are precise. When one breaks, you get the exact rule and the exact state that broke it — not a vague "something's wrong".

Not all invariants are equally hard — and the project says so

A point of intellectual honesty baked into this repo: some invariants are genuinely difficult to keep true and a small bug breaks them; others are nearly true-by-construction. The docs tag every invariant with a class so nobody oversells. There are three:

Fuzz-tensioned

The fuzzer genuinely attacks these. A wrong rounding direction, call order, or missing guard breaks them. The interesting ones. (INV-01, 06, 07–12)

Accounting identity

A bookkeeping equality (sums must match). Holds unless code desyncs the two sides of the ledger. The fuzzer's job here is regression detection. (INV-02, 03)

Structural

True by construction — can't break without editing the source. Kept as cheap regression checks; INV-04 has a non-trivial proof confirmed empirically. (INV-04, 05)

Chapter 06

Proving it before deploy: invariant fuzzing

How do you show 12 rules hold across every sequence, when there are infinitely many? You can't try them all — so you try a colossal number of random ones and watch for any break. That's invariant fuzzing.

The tool here is Foundry. It drives the vault through random call sequences and re-checks all 12 invariants after every single call. To keep the randomness meaningful (instead of wasting runs on calls that just revert), the campaign goes through eight handler contracts — each a thin wrapper exposing one bounded action with sane, randomised inputs.

The eight handlers

Deposit, Collateral, Borrow, Warp (advances time + accrues), Liquidate, Donation (transfers tokens straight to the vault — the share-inflation attack vector), Oracle (moves the price), and Reentrancy. The donation and oracle handlers exist specifically so dangerous attack classes are actually exercised, not just assumed away.

What a green badge means

A precise, falsifiable claim: all 12 invariants held across ~300,000 handler calls without a single assertion failing. If one breaks, Foundry shrinks the failure to the minimal call sequence that reproduces it. A second engine, Echidna, independently re-runs the campaign to corroborate.

An honest limit

Fuzzing is empirical: it shows none of the (hundreds of thousands of) sequences it tried broke a rule. It cannot prove no sequence exists. A formal/symbolic proof (Certora, Halmos) would close that gap — the invariants are deliberately shaped to allow one later — but the repo doesn't ship it yet, and says so plainly.

Chapter 07

The mirror: one rule, both sides of deployment

This is the project's central trick. The 12 invariants proven in CI are re-checked live by a TypeScript monitor — and the monitor is a deliberate 1:1 mirror of the Solidity test, function for function.

Below: the same invariant (INV-01, solvency) in both worlds. Left is the pre-deployment Foundry assertion. Right is the post-deployment monitor in guardian/src/evaluator.ts. They compute the identical boolean. That's the bridge that stops "what we proved" and "what we watch" from drifting apart.

▲ Pre-deploy · Solidity · InvariantVault.t.sol
// asserted after every fuzz call
function invariant_solvency() public {
    uint256 assets = vault.cash()
                   + vault.totalBorrowed();
    assertGe(assets, vault.totalSupplyAssets());
}
▼ Post-deploy · TypeScript · evaluator.ts
// evaluated against live chain state, per block
export function inv01(state: VaultState) {
  const assets = state.cash
               + state.totalBorrowed;
  return { id: 'INV-01',
    passed: assets >= state.totalSupplyAssets };
}

How the monitor sees a live vault

1 · Fetch a full snapshot

Every block (~2s on a real host; ~5min in the free hosted demo), the bot reads the whole vault state in one batched multicall RPC — totals, the borrow index, the oracle reading.

2 · Discover every account

For per-user rules (INV-02/03/06/09/10), it replays the vault's Deposited, Borrowed, Liquidated events to find every address, then reads each one's exact position. It's a real sum over the real users — not a sampled estimate.

3 · Evaluate all 12 — three channels

Snapshot (9 rules): pure function of the current state. Delta (INV-07, 09): compares against the prior block (persisted in Supabase). Event reconciliation (INV-08): re-checks each Liquidated log against that block's oracle price.

4 · Persist & alert

Results land in Supabase (Postgres). A React dashboard reads them via a public read-only key and lights up red the instant a rule breaks.

CI enforces the mirror itself

A dedicated CI job, mirror-parity, fails the build if any invariant_* in the harness lacks a paired check in evaluator.ts. The "write once, enforce both sides" promise isn't a hope — it's a gate.

Chapter 08

Mutation testing: proving the rules actually bite

A green test suite is worthless if the tests can't fail. So the repo deliberately breaks the vault and proves the matching invariant catches it. Each "fuzz-tensioned" rule has a MutantINV*.t.sol partner.

Flip the switch below to introduce a real bug — a broken accrue() that credits lenders twice — and watch the solvency invariant turn red. This is the difference between "the test passed" and "the test would have caught the bug".

The mutation

Canonical vault (correct) 
function accrue() public {
  // ... raise borrowIndex ...
  totalSupplyAssets += interest;
  totalSupplyAssets += interest; // 🐛 credited twice
}

A one-line mutation: lenders are credited interest the vault never collected. The pool now claims to owe more than it holds.

Chapter 09

Exploit replays: historical attacks, re-run

Invariants are abstract. To ground them, the repo replays 10 classes of real-world DeFi exploit against the vault — and against deliberately-broken "attackable" variants — classifying each outcome.

PREVENTED

The contract's own code blocked the attack. No state corruption.

DETECTED

State was corrupted, but an invariant caught it the same block. The monitor's reason to exist.

MISSED

Value extracted, no invariant noticed. A genuine gap — fails the CI build.

The "dual-surface" design is the clever part: each scenario runs against an attackable vault (which has the bug, to prove the attack is real and the check fires) and against the canonical vault (to prove it's actually safe).

Chapter 10

The assurance score (AMC)

An audit produces a static PDF. This project produces a number that CI recomputes on every commit from its own evidence: the Assurance Methodology Coverage score. Crucially, it measures how rigorously the verification methodology was applied — not how secure the code is.

Finding traceability, concretely

The self-review produced 8 findings (GUA-01…08). The tooling checks each is bound to ≥1 invariant and ≥1 harness test and a monitor check. Current: 8/8 fully assured, 0 gaps, 100% weighted coverage. A README sentence is auto-generated from findings.json, and CI fails if anyone hand-edits it out of sync.

The weights are an opinion — stated as one

45/35/20 isn't derived from data; it's an editorial choice documented in the score.ts header. Static verification carries the most weight because it's the broadest evidence. Fixing the weights in the open makes the bias explicit and the score reproducible.

Chapter 12

What this project is not

The single most important chapter for reading it correctly. The repo is unusually forthright about its own limits — that honesty is part of what makes it a serious portfolio piece rather than marketing. Take these at face value.

Not production code

Vault.sol is a teaching example: a permissionless MockOracle (no real price feed), no bad-debt reserve, no governance. Do not custody real value with it.

Not a real audit

security-review/ is written by the repo author — a point-in-time self-review, not an independent third party. Commission a real audit before any real deployment.

The AMC score is self-graded

The same author writes the invariants, picks the exploits, tags the findings, picks the weights, and grades the result. Read "A / 94" as "this codebase grades itself A against the rubric it ships" — reproducible, not externally validated.

Best-effort monitor, not an SLA

The hosted monitor is a free scheduled GitHub Action; GitHub may delay or skip runs, and free-tier RPC limits can fail one. It has never been tested against a real attacker. "Continuous" describes the design pattern, not a hardened service.

The detection demo is staged

AttackableVault.attack() is a planted one-line flag for demonstrating the monitor — not an exploit. The production Vault has no such function.

No formal verification

The proofs are empirical (fuzzing), not symbolic. A Certora/Halmos proof would close the "is there any sequence we missed" question; it's documented as a known, deliberate gap.

The honest one-liner

Guardian Pipeline is a worked, reproducible demonstration of a methodology: define safety once, prove it before deploy, watch the identical definition after deploy, and score the rigor of having done so. It is not a claim that this particular vault is safe to hold your money — and it goes out of its way to say so.
